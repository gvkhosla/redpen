# Memos, proposals, emails, and other written work

Start with the intended reader's decision or action. Adapt to purpose rather than
forcing all writing to sound like a pitch.

Lenses to consider:
- Can the reader find the main point, decision, or ask without excavation?
- Does the reasoning support the conclusion, including meaningful alternatives?
- Are claims distinguished from assumptions and missing evidence?
- Is detail matched to the reader, stakes, and stage?
- Does the structure lower effort, rather than merely shorten the text?
- Are objections addressed where they actually matter?
- Is the proposed action feasible and specific?

Weak: buried ask, vague claims, no path from evidence to conclusion.
Competent: understandable argument, but decision costs or key assumptions remain.
Strong: reader-specific clarity, credible support, honest uncertainty, actionable
next step. A long technical memo can be excellent; brevity is not universal taste.

## Annotated synthetic example
Weak decision close: "Let's discuss this next week."
If the task is to secure a pilot decision, another discussion defers the actual ask.
Stronger direction: "Approve a two-week, one-team pilot using existing tools. We'll
compare meeting time and missed decisions before proposing a rollout."
This narrows the commitment and makes evaluation possible, without fabricating a
result. It is an illustrative direction, not permission to invent owners, dates,
or measurements. If the task were open-ended brainstorming, the discussion close
could be appropriate: task fit outranks a universal preference for decisive copy.

Do not impose a templated executive tone on creative or personal work. Do not
pretend to verify external facts. Suggest research when the supplied evidence is
insufficient. Judge high-stakes professional work provisionally, not as qualified
legal, medical, or financial advice.
